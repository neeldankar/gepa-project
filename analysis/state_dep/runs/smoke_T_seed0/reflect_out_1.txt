Given the input fields:

- `claim` (a statement potentially containing multiple factual components),
- `notes` (a list of extracted information snippets and evidences relevant to the claim from various documents),

your task is to generate the output field:

- `query` — a clear, concise, and focused set of question(s) that, when posed to an information retrieval system or database, would help verify the factual accuracy of the claim.

Guidelines and detailed instructions:

1. **Analyze the claim carefully**:  
   The claim often contains multiple interrelated components or assertions. Break it down conceptually into the minimal factual elements or entities involved (e.g., person-to-person relationships, historical events, attributes of a subject).

2. **Leverage the notes for grounding and gaps**:  
   The notes contain key factual information extracted from sources, including named entities, temporal details, numeric facts, or relationships. They also often mention which relevant documents or facts are missing or partially addressed. Use these notes to:  
   - Identify the key subjects or entities explicitly referenced (e.g., people, places, works, dates, awards).  
   - Detect missing but necessary facts that need confirmation (e.g., "No data found about X" or "Y is unrelated to Z").  
   - Identify domain or niche-specific context, such as literature prizes, film roles, biological taxonomy, or genealogical relations.

3. **Construct the query to address all verification points in the claim**:  
   Your query should:  
   - Directly address the core factual elements that must be verified to establish truth or falsity.  
   - Reflect multi-part claims by splitting queries logically or combining with "and" / "or" clauses as appropriate.  
   - Ensure clarity—avoid ambiguity by specifying exact entities, titles, dates, or relationships mentioned in the claim.  
   - Include explicit questions about relationships, attributes, or status when these are critical to the claim's truth.

4. **Domain-specific considerations identified from examples**:  
   - **Literature domain**: queries often focus on whether a specific person won the Nobel Prize in Literature, or adaptation status of works (e.g., screenplay based on a novel).  
   - **Botany**: comparison of number of species in a genus and verifying vernacular names linked to scientific taxonomy.  
   - **Film and genealogy domain**: verifying familial relations among actors and their filmographies, especially linking parent-child relations to film appearances and release years.  
   - When referencing works, ensure correct disambiguation of similar titles or entities (e.g., distinguishing between "The Night Hunter" and "The Night of the Hunter").

5. **Generalizable strategy**:  
   - Use the notes to identify what is known, what is unknown, and what contradictions or missing data may exist for the claim’s components.  
   - Translate the claim’s components into specific, answerable questions focusing on verifying each claim element or relationship.  
   - If the claim contains comparative statements (e.g., "X is greater than Y"), include queries that fetch attribute values for both X and Y for direct comparison.  
   - If the claim references titles, persons, awards, or other entities with known domain constraints, tailor queries to extract those domain-specific facts explicitly.

6. **Output format**:  
   Output only the `query` field containing the formulated question(s). The query may be multiple questions or a compound question, phrased naturally but precise and explicit.

By following these detailed steps, your generated `query` will be effective for verifying complex factual claims grounded in domain-specific knowledge, facilitating retrieval of the critical evidence required to confirm or refute the claims.
