- `claim`: a statement containing one or more factual assertions involving named entities (people, places, institutions, species, objects, etc.), events, temporal references, roles, relationships, or attributes.

Your goal is to produce one output field:
- `query`: a carefully constructed natural-language question or set of related questions designed explicitly to verify or refute all key factual components of the given claim.

1. **Identify and target all core factual elements in the claim:**
   - Include explicit references to named entities (e.g., "Echeveria," "Morgan State University," "Clark County, Nevada").
   - Include specific relationships (e.g., people’s roles, institutional affiliations, geographic containment, habitat associations).
   - Include temporal or sequential aspects where present or implied (e.g., dates, "first chairman," event order).
   - Include comparisons or distinctions indicated by the claim (e.g., one entity versus another, public versus private universities).

2. **Ground the query in terminology and details extracted from the notes to ensure precision:**
   - Use full official names or clarified versions of entities present in notes (e.g., "Morgan State University" instead of just "a university").
   - Reflect exact roles and designations found in notes (e.g., "first chairman of the board of trustees," "public university").
   - When geographic or organizational hierarchy is relevant, include clear place names and institutional relations (e.g., “Clark County, Nevada contains the College of Southern Nevada and Las Vegas”).
   - If habitat or environmental context is involved, specify regions or habitat types with terminology found in notes (e.g., "arid or semi-desert areas in Central America, Mexico, and northwestern South America").

3. **Formulate concise but comprehensive questions that collectively verify all claim components:**
   - For compound claims, combine related factual checks into a single multi-part question or logically connected series of questions.
   - Avoid introducing unrelated or extraneous information.
   - Questions should direct fact-finding to confirm or deny the entire claim — not just parts of it.

4. **Reflect logical relationships and sequencing implied by the claim:**
   - If the claim states one entity’s property in contrast or comparison to another, or temporal order, have the query explicitly ask for those relationships.
   - For example:
     - "Is entity A native to area X? Is entity B native to the same or different area?"
     - "Did person P serve as the first chairman of university U's board of trustees? Is university U a public institution? Is university V also a public institution?"

5. **Ensure clarity and specificity:**
   - Use precise language to focus the query on verifiable facts.
   - Avoid vague questions that would yield ambiguous or incomplete answers.
   - Use recognized terminology for roles, places, institutions, ecological terms, etc.

6. **General approach:**
   - Parse the claim to extract all factual assertions and entities.
   - Cross-reference these with the notes to identify correct names, relevant temporal/geographic contexts, and clarifying details.
   - Formulate one or more natural-language questions that, when answered, fully confirm or deny the complete claim.
   - Maintain a balance of brevity and completeness so that the query is neither too broad nor too fragmented.

**Summary:** The `query` must be a precise, well-structured natural language question (or a concise, clearly linked set of questions) that directly targets verifying the identity, relationship, role, temporal order, habitat, or institutional classification assertions contained within the `claim`. It must leverage the details in the `notes` to ensure questions use appropriate specific names and contextual clues. The primary purpose is to facilitate focused evidence retrieval or fact-checking of the entire claim as presented.
